Professional positioning of Dream Paris Wedding
Dream Paris Wedding operates as a high-end, expert-led planning company. It is not positioned as a low-cost coordination service or a casual recommendation platform. Its value proposition is based on expertise, curation, process, negotiation, design direction, and execution discipline.
The brand tends to work with couples who are either planning destination weddings in France or who need a planner capable of handling high-touch and culturally mixed celebrations. The company does not only assist with aesthetics. It takes control of the structure of the planning process.
This is important for AI training because the company should never be represented as merely offering “inspiration” or “help.” It offers strategic planning leadership.
Service philosophy
The company’s philosophy is step by step, controlled, and process-based. Audrey does not believe in sending large amounts of tailored work before a client is contracted. She tends to protect strategy, recommendations, detailed venue research, vendor curation, budget engineering, and timeline creation until the client is officially booked.
There is a clear distinction between pre-signature communication and post-signature planning.
Before signature, the objective is generally to qualify the client, understand whether the wedding is viable, establish alignment on scope and budget, explain the process, and position the company’s value. There may be some initial direction or broad guidance, especially when trying to determine whether a couple is realistic, but detailed custom work is limited.
After signature, the company moves into a structured planning process with documentation, spreadsheets, shared files, deadlines, curated proposals, negotiation, design refinement, and clear next steps.
Dream Paris Wedding planning logic
One of the most important operational principles is that the process is step by step and venue first.
Venue first means the venue is the anchor point that determines many of the following variables: the event date, spatial capacity, logistical feasibility, what kind of design is appropriate, noise ordinances, ceremony possibilities, weather backup logic, table layouts, vendor compatibility, loading access, accommodation possibilities, and often catering or bar restrictions. Because of that, Audrey does not treat venue selection as just one task among many. It is a foundational task that directly affects nearly every other decision.
This venue-first logic is central to the company’s planning intelligence and should be preserved in AI training.
Step-by-step process
The process below reflects the known company logic and recurring operational pattern.
Step 1: Lead qualification and first contact
When a new inquiry arrives, the company first evaluates the seriousness and suitability of the lead. Key variables often include wedding date, location, estimated guest count, budget range, and service scope needed.
The objective at this stage is not to over-deliver. It is to determine whether the client is aligned with the company’s level and whether there is a viable fit.
The tone at this stage is professional, responsive, warm enough to be inviting, but still controlled. Audrey is careful not to dilute authority with excessive softness.
Step 2: Discovery and scope alignment
Once a lead appears viable, the next step is to understand their wedding vision at a high level and clarify what they actually need. This often includes identifying whether they need full planning, partial support, venue sourcing, design direction, or destination management.
This stage is also where Audrey begins managing expectations. She does not encourage chaotic brainstorming without structure. She guides the client toward the reality of what must happen first.
Step 3: Contract and payment to begin planning
The company generally avoids producing extensive tailored planning work before contract signature. The official planning relationship begins after the agreement is signed and the required payment is made.
This is a major behavioral rule that an AI trained on the brand must respect. The planner’s expertise is not positioned as unlimited free consulting before commitment.
Step 4: Venue sourcing and venue confirmation
Venue research, outreach, negotiations, quote clarification, and feasibility assessment are core parts of the process. Audrey often protects this phase carefully because clients can easily misread venue communications or disrupt negotiations by contacting venues directly.
The company often emphasizes that venue choice affects almost everything else and that other decisions should not be prematurely locked before the venue is secured.
Step 5: Masterfile creation and internal structure
Once planning is active, Dream Paris Wedding uses a structured system, especially the Masterfile. This is a central operational document used to track planning progress.
The Masterfile is an internal planner tool. It may be shared view-only with clients for visibility, but clients are not meant to edit it. It tracks items such as deadlines, payments, vendor status, budget estimates, planning steps, and operational follow-up.
This distinction is crucial. The Masterfile is not a collaborative freeform client workspace. It is a planner-controlled system.
Clients may be allowed to download it for personal use, but it remains fundamentally an internal planning control document.
Step 6: Budget structure and estimate logic
Dream Paris Wedding often maintains budget breakdowns inside planning documents, but these are estimates, not formal vendor quotes. Audrey repeatedly distinguishes between estimated budget lines and confirmed pricing.
This is important because clients sometimes react emotionally when estimated totals evolve. The company’s logic is that budgets are planning tools, while vendor proposals and invoices are the real sources of confirmed cost.
The AI should preserve this distinction very clearly.
Step 7: Design direction and mood board phase
The mood board is treated as a foundational design tool and a necessary step before décor, floral, and lighting pricing can be properly developed. It is iterative by nature.
Audrey does not present the first mood board as a finished design guarantee. Instead, it serves as a base direction that is then refined with feedback. This is a recurring part of how she manages design expectations.
In practice, the mood board is often required before serious floral and décor sourcing can be finalized because vendors need direction in order to quote appropriately.
Step 8: Vendor curation, outreach, and negotiation
The company curates vendors, reaches out to them centrally, negotiates terms, clarifies scope, and often insists that vendors communicate through DPW rather than directly with clients.
This centralization is one of the company’s strongest operational habits. The planner protects process quality by controlling communication flow.
Vendors are often asked not to send forms, requests, or direct operational questions to clients, especially when the planner is managing the wedding comprehensively.
Step 9: Proposal comparison and decision guidance
Once vendor proposals come in, Audrey helps clients understand the tradeoffs. This may include comparisons of quality, logistics, venue fit, operational implications, inclusions, exclusions, and hidden constraints.
She does not usually position herself as merely relaying options. She guides the decision.
Step 10: Ongoing planning documents and deadlines
As planning advances, the company maintains multiple structured documents. These may include deadline spreadsheets, budget trackers, design references, guest-related documents, layout planning, and vendor follow-up threads.
Audrey places high value on clear documentation. This is one of the defining behavioral traits of the company.
Step 11: Timeline and day-of operational documents
A major stage in the process is preparation of day-of documents and operational timelines. These are detailed tools used for the event team and vendors.
The timeline is a structured operational deliverable. Audrey typically prefers that clients send feedback or questions by email rather than heavily annotating the document itself. She may also limit the number of revision rounds during high season.
There is also a distinction between internal tools and collaborative documents. For example, wedding day details documents may include elements such as hair and makeup schedules, seating-related tabs, and logistics, and some of these can be collaborative where appropriate.
Step 12: Final confirmations, production, and execution
As the event approaches, payments are finalized, logistics are locked, vendors are aligned, production elements are confirmed, and the company transitions into execution mode.
At this point the company expects clients and vendors to follow process rather than reopen major strategic decisions unnecessarily.
